# Types of source: how they differ when extracting a method

## Contents

- A talk, video, or podcast
- A book
- An article
- A course or training programme
- A company regulation
- **A table or matrix**
- **A board, deck, diagram, screenshots**
- Chat logs, tickets, call recordings
- **Forums, reviews, public discussions**
- **Research and academic papers**
- **Code and a working system**
- **Your own notes**
- An interview taken specifically for this
- A summary table

The main file describes the general procedure. Here is what changes depending on where
the material came from.

---

# A talk, video, or podcast

**The strength:** the author qualifies, digresses, gives counterexamples, and admits
boundaries, all of which gets edited out in written preparation. **Asides in speech are more
valuable than the main claim**, because they contain the conditions of applicability.

**The weakness:** there is almost no structure. The order of exposition is dictated by airtime,
not by the logic of application. Don't reproduce it; assemble the sequence anew.

**What to look for specifically:**
- places where the author interrupts themselves ("actually no, I should qualify that…");
- answers to objections from the comments, where the position is stated more precisely than
  in the original claim;
- phrases like "in reality", "in practice", "to be honest", which usually precede something
  that departs from the accepted view.

**The transcript trap:** the phrasings of speech seep into your output unnoticed. After
assembly, reread your own text for the source's characteristic turns of phrase.

**Separately:** the visual part, meaning diagrams, tables, slides, often goes unspoken. Check
which blocks exist only in the picture and request them specifically.

---

# A book

**The strength:** the method is usually structured already, the boundaries thought through,
the examples selected.

**The weakness, and it is more serious than it looks:** the structure of a book is **the
author's and protected**, and it looks so logical that it gets copied almost automatically.
A book's table of contents transferred into a skill's table of contents is direct
reproduction, even when every wording is yours.

**The rule:** the structure of a skill is set by the order of application. A book's order
is set by ease of reading, and those are different things. Coincidence is acceptable where
it is forced (you cannot check a result before doing something), but not as a principle.

**The volume trap:** it is easy to take too much from a book. The selection criterion is
the same: what changes decisions. A chapter from which no action follows does not go
into the skill.

---

# An article

**The strength:** a single claim carried through to the end. Quickly tested for usefulness.

**The weakness:** the length is often padded to satisfy the platform or search results.
Three paragraphs out of thirty may be the useful part.

**How to work with it:** look for **what the author asserts against the accepted view**.
An article restating the consensus contains no method; it popularises one. The difference
is quick to check: is there a single claim a colleague of the author could argue with?

**What to take:** the claim, the justification, the conditions. **What not to take:**
the introduction, restatements of the claim in subheadings, the conclusion. Those are
structural, not substantive.

⚠️ **Check whether it is a retelling.** Articles often set out someone else's method without
naming the source. Working from one of those puts you three hands away from the original. Look
for the primary source if the claim looks well established.

---

# A course or training programme

**The strength:** the material is already broken into steps, often with exercises and marking
criteria, meaning half the work of turning it into a procedure is done.

**The weakness:** a course is built for **learning**, a skill for **execution**. Those are
different jobs, and the structure does not transfer.

| A course has | A skill does not need |
|---|---|
| A motivational part, "why you need this" | A model does not need motivating |
| Gradual progression from simple to complex | The full criterion is needed at once |
| Repetition for memorisation | Repetition takes up space |
| Practice exercises | Only if they are part of the procedure |

**What to take:** the marking criteria. They usually record what separates a correct result
from an incorrect one, and that is exactly the missing criterion.

⚠️ **A course usually carries its own legal regime.** A paid course, a closed community,
subscription access: that is a contract, and it outranks the general rule about the freedom
of ideas. Extracting the method may be forbidden outright by the terms. Check before you start.

---

# A company regulation

**A distinct trap, and the reason a regulation cannot be taken as it stands.**

A regulation looks like a finished procedure, but it describes **how things should be**,
not how they are done. The gap between the two is the real method.

**What to check:**

1. **Whether the regulation is actually followed.** Ask the people working to it. Usually some
   steps have gone unperformed for years without the result suffering, which means those steps
   are redundant in the method.
2. **What people do beyond the regulation.** Undocumented actions that everyone performs are
   the most valuable part: working practice that never made it into the document.
3. **Where the regulation gets bypassed and why.** A bypass signals that the procedure ignores
   a real condition. The condition has to be found and recorded.

**The rule:** a regulation is a hypothesis about a procedure, not the procedure. Test it
against practice before carrying it over.

**On personal data and trade secrets:** an internal regulation almost always contains both.
Names, sums, counterparty names, internal metrics do not get carried over.

---

# A table or matrix

**A special case: a good table is already a method.** Columns are criteria, rows are cases,
the intersection is the decision. The work runs the opposite way from usual: rather than
assembling a procedure from scattered decisions, you **unfold one that is already compressed**.

## The order of analysis

1. **Read the column headings before the contents.** That is the list of criteria the author
   distinguishes cases by, and it is the most valuable part.
2. **Establish what a row is.** Cases, segments, stages, types: this determines what the method
   applies to.
3. **Work out the justification for the columns.** The table does not contain it: the criterion
   is named but not explained. That is what has to be reconstructed.
4. **Check for dead columns.**

| What you see | What it means |
|---|---|
| A column filled identically in every row | The criterion is formal and changes no decision, so it does not enter the method |
| A column empty or half filled | The author intended it but does not use it. Check whether it is needed |
| Two columns that always change together | This is one criterion recorded twice |
| A gap in a row where the others have a value | Either an exception, which is valuable, or unfinished work. Tell them apart from the neighbouring rows |

5. **Find the row that breaks the logic.** Tables almost always contain a case that does not
   fit the grid. That is the boundary of the method.

## What not to do

**Don't carry the table over whole.** The structure of a table is protected, and it is usually
redundant as well: some columns are administrative (number, date, status, owner) and have
nothing to do with the method.

**Don't take completeness for verification.** A forty-row table can be filled in over
one evening on instinct. Ask where the data came from if the author is available.

**Don't lose the sheets.** In working files the instructions, reference data, and examples
often sit on separate tabs, and the method is assembled from all of them. An empty template
sheet carries information too: its structure shows what the author considers mandatory to fill.

## What this source gives that no other does

A ready **list of distinctions**. In a spoken source the criteria have to be fished out one
at a time; here they are written out as a list, because the author has already done the work
of distinguishing the cases.

# A board, deck, diagram, screenshots

**The main limitation:** while the source remains a picture, it stays outside the work.
It cannot be searched by word, checked against what you have written, or quoted exactly.

**The first step is always the same: convert it to text.** Before the analysis, not after.

## What carries meaning in a visual source

| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Arrows and links** | Sequence or dependency, part of the method |
| **Grouping, frames** | What the author treats as one block |
| **Placement** | Usually top to bottom and left to right equals the order of application |
| **Size and emphasis** | What the author considers most important |
| **Notes standing apart** | Caveats and exceptions, often the most valuable part |

**What carries no meaning:** colours, unless a legend is given; decorative elements; placement
dictated by lack of room.

## The order of work

1. **Check whether the material is complete.** A board or a deck rarely arrives whole.
   Work out which blocks are missing and request them specifically, by block name, rather
   than asking for "everything".
2. **Match it against the text**, if a recording of the talk exists. Part of a diagram usually
   gets spoken aloud and part does not. **What went unspoken is what the picture was needed
   for.**
3. **Rewrite it into your own structure.** The diagram cannot be reproduced, since it
   is protected separately from the text. Build the table and the sequence with your own means.
4. **Check the order.** Spatial placement may not match the order of application: on a board,
   blocks go wherever there was room.

## The trap

A visual source looks clearer than text and therefore gets analysed superficially: the diagram
is "visible all at once" and seems obvious. In reality the links between blocks are almost
never labelled, and the links are the method. What an arrow means, "then" or "therefore",
has to be worked out and checked.

# Chat logs, tickets, call recordings

**The strength:** the most honest source there is. Here you see how decisions are actually
made, without rationalisation after the fact.

**The weakness:** the method is not stated at all, only traces of decisions remain. There
is more work here than with any other source.

**How to work with it:** look for **recurring forks**. The same question arriving again and
again with different answers is a ready-made point of the method. From there follow the general
procedure: what differed between the situations.

**What this source gives that no other does:** phrasing in the customer's own language. Direct
speech from tickets and calls cannot be invented or reconstructed.

⚠️ **Personal data is denser here than anywhere.** Names, contacts, sums, case details.
Not a single fragment goes into the file verbatim.

---

# Forums, reviews, public discussions

**The strength:** here you see **the disagreement** that no single-author source contains.
An author presents their position as the only one; a discussion shows there are several,
and along which lines they diverge.

**The weakness:** there is no authority. A confidently stated opinion is indistinguishable
in form from a well-founded one.

## What to look for here

**Not answers but the places where people argue.** A question that keeps starting fights
is a point where the method gives different results under different conditions. It is those
conditions you need to find.

**Phrasing in the language of the people involved.** How they name the problem themselves
is something that cannot be invented.

**Recurring questions.** The same question asked over and over means there is a hole
in the existing methods.

## Telling a well-founded opinion from a confident one

- **a condition is given**: "it worked for me under these circumstances" versus "this works";
- **a counterexample is named**, since someone who knows the subject knows its boundaries;
- **a mechanism is described**, not just a result;
- **failure is admitted**: anyone reporting only successes is not reporting practice.

**The rule:** discussions give **hypotheses and disagreements**, not finished rules. Everything
found here needs confirmation from a source that carries responsibility for its words.

# Research and academic papers

**The only type of source with an explicitly described methodology.** The methods section
is a ready procedure rather than a reconstruction.

**What to take:** the procedure itself, the conditions it ran under, and the limitations,
which the authors usually list themselves. That is the best section for boundaries
of applicability.

**What not to take without thinking:**

| Element | Why to be careful |
|---|---|
| **Figures and effect sizes** | They date, and they depend on the sample. Mark the date and the conditions |
| **Conclusions from the abstract** | Often broader than the data allows |
| **A single study** | One result is not a fact. Check whether it replicated |
| **Popular retellings** | Retellings systematically strengthen conclusions. Go to the original |

**The scale trap:** research runs under controlled conditions, the method gets applied under
uncontrolled ones. The experimental conditions are the boundaries, and they have to be carried
over with the method rather than dropped as a formality.

---

# Code and a working system

**A special case: the method exists and the author may be permanently unavailable**, while
the procedure runs and is visible.

**What it gives:** the actual sequence, undistorted. Code does not rationalise.

**What it does not give:** justification. Why it was done this way and not another does not
follow from the code, and has to be reconstructed by the general procedure.

**Where to look for the method:**

- **conditions and branches**, which are forks, exactly what you need;
- **checks and validation**, the criteria the author considered mandatory;
- **exception handling**, boundaries of applicability in their purest form;
- **constants with comments**, explaining why that particular value;
- **the change history**, showing what was altered and after what. An edit following
  an incident is the most valuable of all.

**The trap:** part of the code is not method but a compromise with circumstances: a workaround
for someone else's bug, a temporary fix that stayed forever. It is distinguishable by comments
and by history. That must not be carried into the method.

---

# Your own notes

The material is yours and the legal regime does not apply, but the extraction is harder than
almost anything else.

**Why:** notes were written for yourself, not for transmission. What they omit is exactly what
seemed obvious to the author, and what is obvious to the author is their method.

**The order:**

1. **Gather everything in one place** and sort by topic rather than by date.
2. **Find the repetitions.** A thought written down three times at different points
   is a settled position, not a mood.
3. **Find what changed.** A note contradicting an earlier one shows the method developing.
   Both versions are valuable: see "Updating an existing method" in the main file.
4. **Fill in what was omitted** by the procedure in "When the expertise is your own".

**The main thing missing from notes is justification.** The conclusion is written down,
the path is not. Reconstruct it through specific cases rather than by trying to remember
what you meant.

# An interview taken specifically for this

The most controllable source: you ask the questions the method needs rather than hunting
for answers inside someone else's structure.

**How to prepare:**

1. Gather what is already known on the topic, so as not to spend time on the general.
2. Find **cases with different outcomes** in the expert's practice in advance. The main
   technique is built on them.
3. Prepare questions about boundaries and about negative rules, which the expert will never
   volunteer.

The questioning technique is in the main file, section "When you can ask the expert".

**What to write down right after the conversation, before it fades:** the places where
the expert hesitated or did not answer at once. Hesitation usually means the criterion exists
but is not formulated, and it is worth going back there on a second pass.

**The legal regime:** an interview taken from a person is a joint result. Agree the terms
of use before publication, not after. If the expert does not want to appear, the method stays
and the attribution comes off at their request.

# If there are several sources

The normal case for a real skill.

**Take them one at a time and combine only afterwards.** Trying to read them in parallel
produces a blend: it becomes unclear whose criterion it is and under what conditions it was
stated.

**Record the type and scale of each source.** A rule from a solo worker and a rule from
a company of a hundred can sound identical and mean different things.

**When they diverge, use the procedure from the main file**, section "Contradictions
in the material". Don't pick a winner by the authority of the source: the frame matters more
than the credentials.

**Check whether they are retelling each other.** Three articles with one claim may all trace
back to a single original, which is one confirmation rather than three.
